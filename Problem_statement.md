# AptoHire: Making skills visible beyond resumes

*Problem statement, full version*

## 1. Summary

In hiring today, a resume and a college name often count for more than what a person can actually do. Skilled students, especially from Tier-2 and Tier-3 colleges, get overlooked because they have no trusted way to prove their ability. Employers have the opposite problem: checking real skills is slow and costly, and it is getting harder as AI floods hiring with polished applications. Universities, in the middle, cannot see how practical their students' skills are or how well they match what industry needs.

This document explains the problem, shows the evidence that it is real, describes who is affected, and states the challenge AptoHire sets out to solve.

> **The core challenge:** How might we make practical skills verifiable, talent discoverable, and hiring more evidence-based, while protecting student privacy and reducing the cost of skill assessment?

## 2. The problem in plain words

**For students.** A student can build real things and still have a resume that looks like everyone else's. Without a famous college name or a personal contact, their application is easy to skip. They also often don't know which skills to build next, or how good their work really is.

**For employers.** A recruiter reading "proficient in React" cannot tell whether that means one tutorial or two years of real work. To find out, they run tests, review projects and hold repeated screening rounds. This takes time, and every employer repeats the same checks from scratch.

**For universities.** A training and placement office can see grades and, later, who got placed. It usually cannot see which practical skills its students actually have, or which skills employers keep asking for that students don't yet supply.

**What exists today.** Job portals connect people to jobs. Assessment platforms test people for one opening at a time. But a gap remains between three things: the work a student actually produces, believable proof of that ability, and the hiring decision that follows.

## 3. Why this is a real problem: the evidence

The numbers below come from public reports and surveys. Each one is linked in the Sources section at the end.

### 3.1 Hiring is drowning in applications

Applying has become almost effortless, partly because of AI tools. Recruiters now sit under a flood.

- LinkedIn has reported about **11,000 job applications every minute**, up **45%** in a year, with AI-powered apply tools a big driver.
- Jobs on Greenhouse received about **244 applications each in 2025**, up **111%** since 2022. Greenhouse also reported that applications handled per recruiter rose by about **204%**, while the average number of recruiters per company fell by more than half.
- In Greenhouse's 2025 AI in Hiring Report, **77%** of hiring teams said they regularly see AI-generated or AI-assisted applications, and **34%** of recruiters said they spend up to half their working week filtering spam and junk applications.

More applications do not mean more good candidates. They mean that good candidates are harder to find, and that a capable student with an ordinary-looking resume is easier to miss.

### 3.2 Resumes and profiles are harder to trust

When anyone can produce a polished resume in minutes, the resume tells employers less.

- Gartner predicts that by **2028, one in four candidate profiles worldwide could be fake**. In its survey of about 3,000 candidates, **6%** admitted taking part in interview fraud, such as posing as someone else.
- Greenhouse reported that **91%** of US recruiters had spotted candidate deception, according to a roundup of its 2025 data.
- Gartner also found that only about **a quarter of candidates** trust AI to evaluate them fairly, while around **four in ten** said they use AI themselves in the application process.

Trust is falling on both sides. Employers doubt what candidates claim, and candidates doubt how employers judge them. Honest students pay for this doubt too.

### 3.3 Employers say they want skills, but practice lags

Many employers now say skills matter more than degrees. Proving skills is still hard.

- TestGorilla's 2025 survey of 1,084 hiring decision-makers in the UK and US found that **85%** of employers say they use skills-based hiring (up from 81% the year before) and **53%** have dropped degree requirements (up from 30%). Employers in that survey said skills tests predict job success better than resume screening.
- Yet a Harvard Business School and Burning Glass Institute study found that dropping degree requirements on paper rarely changed who got hired: the extra opportunity reached **fewer than 1 in 700 hires**, and most of the real change came from just **37%** of firms. The study concluded that most companies' commitment stays at the level of policy.

Our reading: employers are willing, but they lack an easy, trusted way to see real skills. Where evidence is missing, familiar shortcuts such as college name, resume formatting and personal referrals take over.

### 3.4 In India, being job-ready does not mean being found

- The **India Skills Report 2026** (ETS and Wheebox with CII, AICTE, AIU and Taggd; more than 1 lakh candidates and 1,000 employers) found **56.35%** of tested candidates employable, up from 54.81% in 2025 and 46.2% in 2022. Among BE/BTech graduates it was **70.15%**, and among computer science graduates **80%**. The report also noted Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities such as Lucknow, Kochi and Chandigarh emerging as strong employability hubs.
- The government's **PLFS Annual Report 2025** (Ministry of Statistics) shows unemployment among **15 to 29 year olds at 9.9%**, more than three times the overall rate of **3.1%**. Among educated persons (secondary level and above) it was **6.5%**, about double the overall rate. A summary of the same report puts urban youth unemployment at **13.6%**.
- The **India Employment Report 2024** (ILO and the Institute for Human Development) found that young people make up about **83%** of India's unemployed, and that the share of educated youth (secondary or higher) among the unemployed youth rose from **35.2% in 2000 to 65.7% in 2022**. In 2022 the youth unemployment rate was reported as **29.1% for graduates**, compared with 3.4% for youth who could not read or write.
- More recently, Business Today reported (July 2026) that the monthly PLFS for June 2026 showed youth unemployment at **16.2%** against 5.5% overall, and cited the State of Working India 2026 report as finding that nearly 40% of young graduates in the labour force reported open unemployment.

These figures measure different things and use different years and methods, so they should not be compared directly. A skills test measures readiness. Labour surveys measure outcomes. Read together, they point to the same gap: many ready, educated young people are not being matched to opportunity.

### 3.5 The scale is large

According to the All India Survey on Higher Education (AISHE) 2021–22, India has **1,168 universities, 45,473 colleges and about 4.33 crore (43.3 million) students**. Most of them study outside the few institutions whose names carry weight with employers.

### 3.7 Real-world signals

Statistics show the size of the problem. Stories show how it feels.

- **A widely shared LinkedIn post.** An HR professional described rejecting a candidate's CV twice. On the third application they finally called him for an interview, offered him the role straight after, and a year later he was a Team Leader performing exceptionally well. Their lesson: never judge a person only by their CV, because potential does not always fit on one page. The post has more than **22,000 likes, 626 comments and 913 reposts**. This is a signal, not a statistic. It shows how strongly people recognise the problem, not how often it happens. It also shows what the story needed to work: a recruiter who happened to give the candidate a third chance. A system should not depend on luck.
- **A reference video titled "Getting a Job in 2026 is a Joke."** Its visuals show the same themes as the data: a flood of applications for one role, AI tools that apply to jobs automatically, and candidates using AI to get through interviews. We use it as context, not as a source of numbers.

How to read the evidence: the figures come from different countries, years and methods. The India figures are the most relevant for AptoHire's first users. The global hiring figures show that the problem is growing and that AI is accelerating it.

## 4. Who is affected

| Who | What they want | What gets in the way | What it costs them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Students** (Tier-2 and Tier-3 focus, tech and management) | A job or internship that fits their ability | Resume is ignored; no trusted proof; unclear what to learn next | Talent goes unseen; lost confidence; poor-fit roles |
| **Employers** (corporates, start-ups) | Skilled hires at low cost | Unverified claims; flooded inbox; slow manual checks | High cost per hire; good candidates missed; bad hires |
| **Universities** (placement cells, departments) | Strong placements and reputation | No data on practical skills or employer demand | Training guided by guesswork; placement relies on a few contacts |

## 5. Why current solutions fall short

These options are useful. The point is not that they are bad. The point is that none of them, alone, creates trusted, student-owned evidence that employers can search without bias and that students can share on their own terms.

| Option | What it does well | Why it does not solve this problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Job portals and professional networks** | Distribute jobs and profiles at very large scale | Built on self-declared profiles and keyword matching; they connect people but do not produce evidence |
| **Skill-assessment platforms** | Test candidates objectively when an employer asks | Usually tied to one employer's test, not a lasting, student-owned profile that grows over time |
| **Competition and hiring platforms** | Combine contests, tests and hiring for a campus audience | Partly overlapping; AptoHire centres evidence from real projects, focuses on Tier-2 and Tier-3 students, and uses consent-based blind discovery |
| **Open portfolios** (GitHub, Kaggle, Behance) | Show real work in public | Unverified and unstructured; hard to compare by skill and level |
| **Campus placement** | Direct access to visiting employers | Depends on the college's brand and the placement officer's network |
| **Referrals and agencies** | Trust through a personal link or a paid screen | Favour people who already have connections; agencies add cost |

The gap is the combination of four things: evidence produced from real work, a record that belongs to the student for life, discovery that does not reveal identity before consent, and visibility for the university.

## 6. Root causes

1. **Evidence is not a by-product of learning.** Students build things, but the work is not turned into a structured, checkable record.
2. **Reviewing work does not scale.** Skilled human reviewers are expensive, so review either does not happen or happens late, for a few candidates.
3. **Trust and privacy pull in opposite directions.** Employers want proof. Students do not want to expose their identity to bias, spam or misuse before they choose to. There is no widely used, consent-first way to do both.
4. **The signals that get used are biased.** Names, institutions and networks stand in for ability.
5. **Records follow institutions, not people.** When a student changes university, drops out, graduates or moves on to a master's degree, their history fragments and they re-enter everything.
6. **AI has removed the cost of looking impressive.** When polished resumes and cover letters are free, self-declared claims lose value, and verification becomes the scarce thing.

## 7. The problem statement

> **Skilled students from Tier-2 and Tier-3 colleges cannot prove what they can do in a way employers trust, and employers cannot verify skills without slow, costly manual checks. Universities lack reliable evidence of their students' practical skills and of how those skills relate to employer demand and placement. There is no trusted, portable, evidence-based record of capability that respects student privacy and consent.**

### How might we...

- ...turn the work students do into **structured, checkable proof** at a cost students and colleges can afford?
- ...let employers **find and trust** talent on evidence, **without revealing identity** until the student agrees?
- ...give every student **one permanent profile** that follows them through every change, with no double entry?
- ...show universities **which practical skills their students have**, which skills the market wants, and how this connects to placement?
- ...keep **AI fast and fair**, with humans involved wherever a decision is borderline, contested or sensitive?

### Scope

**In scope:** skill verification through real projects with evidence-linked scores; a proof-based digital resume and public verification page; blind, consent-first matching; university analytics (roster, verification, skills, placements, unmatched employer demand); continuity across university changes, graduation and dropout; student appeals and human review for borderline results.

**Out of scope:** guaranteeing a job or a shortlist (recruiters decide; AptoHire promises better visibility backed by proof); replacing interviews or a recruiter's judgment; real payments and integrations with college or recruiting systems in the prototype; public rankings of universities.

**Where we start:** tech students and one skill family, Web and Frontend development, because code can be checked against clear criteria. Management and commerce skills are part of the long-term audience, but they are more subjective and need more human-reviewed rubrics.

## 8. How AptoHire responds

AptoHire is an AI-first skill-verification and hiring platform. Each part answers a problem above.

| Problem | What AptoHire does |
| --- | --- |
| Resumes are self-declared | Students complete real projects. An AI review scores the work against a rubric and links every score to its **evidence**. Skills above the pass mark (50 by default) become **Verified** |
| Checking skills is slow and costly | The first review is automated, so results can arrive in minutes. Borderline scores (40 to 60) go to **human review**, and students can **appeal** |
| Students don't know what to build | A **project assistant** suggests a real-world project at the right level, with a clear rubric |
| Real work is unstructured | A **living skill scorecard** and a **digital resume** with a QR code to a public verification page that shows only what the student chooses to share |
| Proxies and bias | **Blind, consent-first matching**: employers see skills, levels, reasons and confidence, not names. Identity is revealed only after the student accepts, and access can be withdrawn at any time |
| Fragmented records | **One permanent profile** across university changes, graduation and dropout; scores never reset |
| Universities lack data | A **dashboard** with roster, verification, the path from invited to verified, skills supply versus employer demand, placements, and unmatched-demand signals (counts and reasons, never identities) |
| Trust in the platform | **Admin governance**: approvals, review queues, an audit log and an AI kill switch |

### Why AI, and why not only AI

AI is needed for work that does not scale by hand: reviewing many project submissions against a rubric, suggesting level-appropriate projects, reading job descriptions into required skills, and explaining results in plain language. Given the application flood described above, no team could do this manually.

But AI is **not** used for identity checks, consent, permissions or payments. Those are rule-based. The principle is **AI proposes, rules decide**. AI does the first pass, humans handle borderline results and appeals, and the platform tracks how often AI and human reviewers agree, with an initial target of at least 80%.

## 9. Principles

- **Evidence over claims.** Every score points to the work that earned it.
- **Explainable.** Students and employers see why a result or match was produced, with a confidence level, not just a number.
- **Fair by design.** The work is judged first. Accent, speaking pace, fluency, camera quality and background are not scored. Disclosing AI use is not penalised. Missing evidence lowers confidence instead of lowering the score.
- **Private and consent-first.** Employers never see identity before the student says yes. Students choose what is shared, for how long, and can withdraw.
- **No double entry.** One profile for life.
- **Honest promises.** AptoHire improves visibility with verified proof. It does not guarantee outcomes, and a match is a recommendation with reasons, not a prediction of hiring success.
- **Low cost.** The prototype runs on free tiers, and every AI step has a fallback so it can be demonstrated reliably.

## 10. How we will know it works

### Prototype goals

| Goal | Done when |
| --- | --- |
| Student "magic moment" | A student submits a project and sees an evidence-linked scorecard and a verified badge within minutes |
| Blind, consent-first matching | An employer posts a job, sees anonymous ranked candidates with reasons, requests access, and the student accepts; identity is revealed only then |
| University value | A dashboard shows roster, verification, skills and placement analytics |
| No double entry | Students move through alumni, dropout and master's paths across registered and unregistered universities without losing scores |
| Separate portals | The root site and the student, university, organization and admin portals each run on their own address and share one database |
| Near-zero cost | The whole demonstration runs on free tiers, with a mock or fallback mode for every AI step |

### Pilot hypotheses

A first real-world pilot would be small: two universities, one IT or computer science department, 50 to 100 students, one skill family and three to five verified employers. These are targets to test, not claims:

- At least **60%** of onboarded students submit a project.
- At least **30** projects are assessed.
- AI and human reviewers **agree within one level on at least 80%** of a 30-project check.
- Each employer **views at least five candidates**.
- At least **50%** of access requests are **accepted** by students.

## 11. Assumptions to validate and limits of the evidence

The problem is well supported, but the solution rests on assumptions that real users should confirm or correct.

1. **Employers value verified proof enough to change how they screen**, and eventually to pay for it. Test with 10 to 15 recruiters.
2. **Universities want practical-skill and placement analytics.** Test with about five placement officers, including what they would pay for.
3. **Students will complete a project and record a short demo video** and find it worth the effort.
4. **AI reviews can be accurate and fair enough to be useful**, with humans handling edge cases. The agreement target above is the test.
5. **Students will accept consent-based discovery**, and employers will accept not seeing names up front.
6. **A proof-based resume is trusted more than an ordinary one.** In practice recruiters will still ask for a resume or links, so the proof must travel with them.

Limits of the evidence in this document:

- Many figures are from surveys or reports run by companies that sell hiring products, and several reach us through news or summary pages. They should be checked against the original reports before formal use.
- Global hiring data (United States, United Kingdom and worldwide) may not match Indian campus hiring. India-specific figures are included wherever we found them.
- Employability scores and unemployment rates measure different things and are not directly comparable.
- The LinkedIn post and the video are signals of how widely the problem is felt, not measurements of how often it happens.
- Revenue ideas (a university licence fee, student access tiers, and employer subscriptions) are hypotheses for later. The prototype implements no payments, and no price has been validated.

## 12. Conclusion

Today, a student's ability stays invisible until someone spends time checking it. That cost is rising as AI floods hiring with applications that all look polished, and the students most likely to be overlooked are those without a famous college or a personal network. Employers pay with slow screening and bad hires. Universities pay with weak, unmeasured training. The statistics, a viral post from a recruiter who nearly missed a future Team Leader, and a video about getting a job in 2026 all describe the same gap.

AptoHire's answer is to make proof the default: real projects, reviewed quickly and fairly, linked to evidence, owned by the student, and shared only with consent. If it works, a student in a small town and a student at a famous institute can be seen for what they can do, and an employer can find them without having to guess.
